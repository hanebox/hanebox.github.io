title: Why Most Linux UIs Still Suck
description: I keep wanting to love desktop Linux, but making it feel coherent is still far more expensive than it should be.
date: 2026-08-03 10:00:00 +0700
---

I keep wanting to love desktop Linux. I like what it stands for, I like having control over my own machine, and I like that there is usually more than one way to do something. Then I open an app and run into a strange dialog, inconsistent spacing, or a button that behaves differently from the one beside it. The spell breaks a little.

I do not think this happens because Linux developers do not care. A huge amount of thoughtful, mostly unpaid work goes into the desktop. The problem, as I see it, is that making something feel good on Linux is unusually expensive.

The people who sit between design and engineering, what we now tend to call design engineers, usually care deeply about simplicity. They notice when a system has too many concepts, too many exceptions, or too much history leaking into the experience. They know that every extra layer creates more edge cases and more decisions to revisit later.

Linux GUI work asks them to inherit that complexity on day one. There is GTK and Qt, different desktop environments, different window managers, distro-specific choices, old behavior that cannot simply disappear, and conventions that change depending on where an app is running. None of these things is unreasonable on its own. Together, they turn small design decisions into negotiations with an entire ecosystem.

I understand why many people choose the web or mobile instead. Those platforms have plenty of problems, but their boundaries are clearer. A person or small team can still shape most of an experience, ship it, and quickly see whether it works. The effort has a visible payoff. On Linux, polishing one corner can reveal five more layers that also need attention.

That creates an unfortunate loop. The people most sensitive to coherence are also the least willing to keep adding complexity. Many move somewhere their attention has more leverage. The people who remain do excellent work making software function across a messy environment, but function and feel do not always receive the same amount of time.

I still want the Linux desktop to succeed. I want open platforms to feel as considered as the closed ones, not merely because polish looks nice, but because good design makes software easier to trust and easier to use. Until creating a thoughtful Linux interface becomes less costly, though, I think many of its UIs will continue to feel almost finished rather than truly complete.
